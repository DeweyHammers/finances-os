Finances OS

Plan every paycheck against every bill.

A free desktop app that turns your paydays into a week-by-week plan, so rent, car payments and next month's bills are covered before they're due, and you can see exactly what's left to spend.

Take the tour → View on GitHub

Pay-week view

Every month is split into the weeks between your paydays, with income, bills and what's left over for each one.

One-click Optimize

Big bills get split across several paychecks so no single week gets crushed, and every week stays above your surplus target.

Try it in a sandbox

Wondering what cancelling a bill would do? Switch to a copy of your data, change anything, re-optimize, then keep it or throw it away.

Zero-based Plan

Give every dollar a job. Auto-assign funds each bill and allowance with exactly what this payday owes it.

Private by design

Runs on your computer with a local database. No account, no cloud, no bank logins.

Meet Alex

This tour starts from a completely empty install and follows one made-up person, Alex, through their first month with Finances OS. Every name and number below is fictional.

Alex installs the app on Thursday, October 1st. Payday is every Friday.

💵 $1,150 every Friday 🧾 8 monthly bills · $2,104 🛒 4 everyday expenses 🐷 $100/week to savings 📅 4 yearly costs 🏦 $1,800 in checking
01

Download & install

GitHub → Releases

Finances OS is a Windows app. Download the latest Finances OS Setup x.x.x.exe from the Releases page and run it; the installer lets you pick a folder and adds a desktop shortcut. Because the app isn't signed with a paid certificate, Windows SmartScreen may show a blue “Windows protected your PC” box the first time: click More info, then Run anyway.

Why it matters: there's nothing else to set up. No account to create and no bank to connect; everything the app knows lives in a database file on your own PC.
02

A fresh start

First launch

A brand-new install has no data at all. Instead of blank tables, every page tells you what it's for and what to do first.

Why it matters: you never have to guess where to begin. The Overview points you to your income, since everything else is planned around your paydays.
03

Add your income

Income → Add Your First Income

Enter what you take home each paycheck, how often you're paid, and on which day. Alex gets $1,150 weekly on Fridays.

Why it matters: your paydays build the pay-week grid. October has five Fridays, so Alex's October has five pay weeks, and every bill gets assigned to the paycheck that should cover it.
04

Add your bills

Bills → Create New Bill

Add each monthly bill with its amount and the day it's due. Alex has eight, from $1,400 rent on the 1st down to a $16 streaming plan on the 27th.

Why it matters: due dates are what let the app work out which paycheck pays for what. Tick “Never split” for a bill that must be paid in one go from a single paycheck.
05

Everyday spending

Personal → Create New Personal

Personal expenses are the money you spend (or set aside) as you go. Every Pay Week gives a fresh allowance from each paycheck: groceries, gas, fun money, and Alex's $100 savings transfer. Split Monthly spreads one monthly total across the weeks (household supplies).

Why it matters: once allowances are set aside, the surplus you see each week is money you can actually spend or save. It isn't already promised to groceries.
06

Yearly costs

Yearly Costs → Create New Yearly Cost

Some bills only show up once a year: car registration, a club membership, holiday gifts. Add them with the month and day they're due.

Why it matters: the costs that hurt most are the ones you forget. The Overview lays them out by month so you can see them coming.
07

Overview & Optimize

Overview → Optimize Now

The Overview now shows October as five pay weeks: income, expenses and the surplus left in each. Before optimizing, every bill sits in the week it's due. November's $1,400 rent lands in Pay Week 5, leaving Alex $595 short that week.

One click on Optimize spreads big bills across earlier paychecks and plans the next three years. Rent is now saved a little each week, and every week clears the $300 surplus target. That $300 a week is just the default: change it any time with the pencil on the Surplus Target pill at the top of the Overview.

Why it matters: you see a bad week coming weeks ahead, and the fix is already planned. Split cards show how much of a bill has been saved so far.
08

Add your bank account

Sidebar → Cash → Add Account

Add a checking account with its current balance. Alex starts with $1,800. Balances are always calculated from the transactions, so they never drift.

Why it matters: your real money is what the Plan hands out. The starting balance becomes your first “Ready to Assign” dollars.
09

Build your Plan

Plan → Create your first group

The Plan is a zero-based budget: every dollar gets a job. Create groups (Alex uses Bills, Everyday and Goals) and fill them with your bills and personal expenses, including Alex's $100-a-week Savings.

Why it matters: items linked to your bills and allowances know their own amounts and schedules, so the Plan can fund them for you on payday.
10

Payday: Auto-assign

Plan → Assign → Auto

Friday, October 2nd: payday. Alex records the paycheck in Cash → Checking → Add Transaction: an inflow for the paycheck amount, the memo Income, and no category. That lands the money in Ready to Assign, and the memo is what tells the app a paycheck has arrived. Then, in the Plan, Assign → Auto funds this pay week in one click: each allowance gets its weekly amount, and each bill gets exactly the slice the Optimize schedule set for this week.

Why it matters: no math on payday. The Plan and the Overview always agree, and an amber ⚠ appears if an item ever drifts from the schedule.
11

Track your spending

Cash → Checking

Through October, Alex logs purchases and bill payments in the checking ledger, each one tagged with a Plan item. Every Friday: record the paycheck, then click Auto.

Why it matters: each item's Available balance shows what's really left in that envelope, so overspending shows up the same day, not at the end of the month.
12

What if? Try a sandbox

Overview → Try a sandbox

At the end of the month Alex wonders: what would next month look like without the gym? Try a sandbox switches the whole app onto a copy of the data. An amber banner stays on every page while it's active. Alex deletes the gym membership, clicks Optimize, and flips to November to see the result, all without touching the real numbers.

When you're done, Discard throws the copy away and the app goes straight back to your real data. If you like what you see, Keep makes it real instead (a backup of the old data is saved first). Closing the app always discards the sandbox.

Why it matters: you can test a decision before you make it, and a wrong experiment costs nothing.
13

Look back

Statistics · Overview

Statistics breaks spending (and income) down by item and payee, month by month and across the year. Click any bar to jump into that month.

Why it matters: a plan is only as good as what actually happened. Seeing where October really went makes November's plan better.

Finances OS is a Windows desktop app (Electron + Next.js + SQLite).

Everything in this tour (names, amounts, payees) is made up. The screenshots are generated automatically from an empty demo database by scripts/demo/tour.mjs, so they always match the current app.
